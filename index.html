Case 1
Fitness app · retention

PureFit — What Metric Improves Retention?

Problem statement

PureFit's app offers fitness videos, exercise how-tos, diet plans, plus live classes and sessions to join.

Free customers

Access to certain videos and information — nothing customizable.

Paid customers

Personalized diet plans and expert advice tied to their fitness goals.

Definition of "retained": a user who has stayed with us for at least two months. Fall off before that, and they don't count as retained.

🧩 Analogy
🪣
Retention = a leaky bucket
Pouring in more users is pointless if they leak out the sides
Retention is the water that stays after two months. If you only measure acquisition, you're pouring faster into a leaking bucket. Define a metric at every earlier stage so you can find — and patch — the holes before they cost you retention.

Quiz 1 · Funnel thinking & retention
"Retention" means a user staying active for at least two months — a metric that only appears very late in the funnel. Why is it still smart to define metrics at the earlier stages (acquisition, onboarding, engagement)?
Answer: B. Retention is a lagging outcome — by the time it dips, the users are already gone. Defining metrics at each earlier stage exposes where users leak sooner, so you can act upstream. That's the leaky-bucket idea: plug the holes before pouring in more users.

A metric at every stage

Retention comes late in the funnel, so we define metrics across every stage to expose the drop-off points and design strategies to boost it.

1

Number of new users joined / Sign-ups

Acquisition
  • The most basic metrics — direct read on the product's ongoing statistics and the first step of the funnel
  • In a growth phase, the main goal is often growing the user base and getting more people onboard
  • Feeds strategy: adjust marketing, and build a notification system to nudge onboarded users to attend more classes/videos and stay active
2

Retention table / Nth-day retention

Retention
  • N-day retention = how many users come back on a particular day, counting from Day 0
  • Day 0 = the day a free user downloaded the app, or a paid user made their transaction
  • High new-user counts mean little if retention is low — you'll keep losing customers and shrink your base
  • It's a direct read on product quality and stickiness
Worked example
1000
Day 0
130
Day 7

Day-7 retention = 130 / 1000 = 13%. Day 0 is each user's own start date — which is why cohorts are tracked per acquisition date.

Quiz 2 · N-day retention
A cohort of 1,000 users first opens the app on Day 0. On Day 7, 130 of them open it again. What is this cohort's Day-7 retention, and what does "Day 0" represent?
Answer: A. N-day retention = (users active on day N) / (users who first used the app on Day 0) = 130 / 1000 = 13%. Day 0 is each user's own starting point (a free user's download or a paid user's first transaction), not one fixed calendar date — which is why cohorts are tracked per acquisition date.
3

Classes attended / duration of class attended

Engagement
  • Total classes attended and time logged in — plus when they drop off (e.g. signing in, browsing, leaving within 2–3 min → fix search & recommendations)
  • Weekly / Monthly active users (WAU/MAU) also belong here — how many of the users we acquired are actually using the app in a given week or month
  • Low "watched till the end" numbers suggest users don't like the content
  • Define the desired interaction level to measure against it
Super 🎉

More than once a day

Good ✅

At least once a day

Bad ⚠️

Less than twice a week / once a week

4

Referrals & links shared / feedback received

Retention
  • Heavy sharing of a video or diet plan = users find it useful and love it
  • Powers a referral-based marketing strategy (perks/discounts for connections who join)
  • Feedback ("How was the session?", how the user communicates) → analyse to gauge whether the user will stay
5

Match rate between user and content

Engagement

Really an onboarding problem: if we don't capture a user's goal and fitness level at sign-up, we serve mismatched content from day one. Measure it with:

Match rate

% of new users whose first few classes match the goal & level they selected at sign-up

Onboarding completion rate

% of new sign-ups who complete the goal/level questionnaire

  • Why is the user dropping off? Beyond raw content quality, are they getting what they came for?
  • A beginner signed up for advanced content will be disheartened and drop off; someone wanting Zumba led into yoga/weight-training won't stay
  • Fix: tag & categorise all classes by level and type; ask users their intent and expertise up front to recommend a perfect match

Note: a metric like free-to-paid conversion % would matter if the goal were the success of the overall app — not retention specifically. Always tie the metric back to the stated goal.

Case 2
Banking app · success

Indian Bank — Defining Success for a Bank App

Problem statement

Indian Bank has run for 5 years with an established customer base, and just launched a new mobile app. What success metrics determine the app's success?

  • Scope: focus on the success of the mobile app only, not the whole bank
  • Two user types: existing users who port to the app, and new users joining via it (new users add an account-creation step)
  • After that, the journey is the same for everyone: transactions and features — investments, fixed & recurring deposits, loans, etc.
Split A · Revenue-based

Revenue generated or routed through the app — the direct business impact of its features.

Split B · Adoption (non-revenue)

Is the app user-friendly and meeting user expectations?

🧩 Analogy
🚪
New revenue vs shifted revenue
A new door for the same crowd isn't a bigger crowd
₹50 crore flowing through the app looks like new money — but if it's the same customers who used to walk into a branch, you've just opened a shinier door. The building isn't fuller; people came in a different way. Separate genuinely new revenue from merely shifted revenue.

The Cannibalization check from TROPiCS

1. Revenue-based metrics

To measure the app's revenue, first understand how the bank itself earns — then isolate the app's slice.

How banks make money
Credit card feesChecking accountsSavings accounts Mutual fund revenueInvestment management feesPayment gateway fees
Revenue up from less friction

Easier online payments, loans and investments pull in users who never used those features before. Paying via wallet/UPI is far easier than internet banking — so overall revenue rises.

Revenue from the app itself

To isolate the app's true impact, separate existing-channel revenue from app revenue (mind cannibalization). Target:

Transactions per dayTransactions per customerTotal transaction value

Also slice by transaction type, merchant and purpose to attribute correctly.

Target question: what share of transactions via the app signals success? Assuming an industry shift to online, you could target roughly 80% of transactions and feature access for a successful transition to a mobile-oriented bank.

Quiz 3 · New revenue vs shifted revenue
The app reports ₹50 crore in monthly transactions. Before calling the app a revenue success, why must you separate this from the bank's existing channel revenue?
Answer: B. Much of the app's volume can be existing customers doing the same activity through a new channel — channel shift, not incremental revenue. Isolate genuinely new revenue (new users, new behaviours) from merely cannibalized revenue — the same Cannibalization check you met in TROPiCS.

2. User adoption metrics

Money through the app is half the picture. The other half: are people actually adopting it?

Segment adoption two ways
  • Existing users who ported to the mobile app
  • New users joining via the app

This also helps attribute future app-generated revenue.

Accounts opened via app

Measure total accounts vs accounts now opened through the app. The ratio is what you push up — telling you how comfortable users are doing tasks (account opening, FDs, investments) on the app.

3. User feedback & application records

NPS

Net Promoter Score — one of the best reads on long-term growth.

Sentiment analysis

On user feedback — surfaces problems and the user's perspective.

Abandon Rate

% who start a process in the app but don't complete it — where & why they drop.

Task Completion Rate

The mirror image — are users achieving their intended tasks, and at what rate?

📉 Interactive: abandon-rate flow drop-off ↗
Quiz 4 · Choosing the right feedback metric
Many users start a fund transfer but quit before confirming it. Which metric most directly captures this specific problem?
Answer: B. Abandon Rate is the % of users who start a process but don't complete it — exactly "start a transfer, drop before confirming," and it pinpoints where in the flow they quit. NPS measures overall loyalty, not a specific drop-off; downloads and MAU are volume metrics that say nothing about task failure. (Task Completion Rate is the mirror image — the % who succeed.)
Interview corner

Practice Questions

Frame your own answer first — then reveal the approach.

Q1 — Improve retention for a language-learning app. What would you measure?
Funnel-stage metrics
Describe + goal: define "retained" (e.g. active 30+ days); the goal is repeat practice.
Walk the funnel: Acquisition (installs) → Onboarding (first lesson completed) → Engagement (lessons/day, streaks, N-day retention) → Retention (30-day return).
Match rate: is content at the learner's level? A beginner in advanced lessons churns.
Leaky bucket: find the earliest stage that leaks and fix it before buying more installs.
Q2 — A retailer launches a payments app. How do you measure success?
Revenue vs Adoption
Split it: revenue metrics (transactions/day, per customer, total value) + adoption metrics (ported vs new users, accounts/wallets created via app).
Cannibalization: separate genuinely new revenue from card/branch revenue that just shifted channels.
Experience: NPS, sentiment, Abandon Rate and Task Completion Rate to catch broken flows.
Q3 — Users complete a purchase but 40% abandon before payment. Which metric, and what next?
Feedback metric
Metric: Abandon Rate — % who start but don't finish; pinpoints the exact step they quit.
Mirror: Task Completion Rate to track improvement as you fix the flow.
Then: segment abandonment by step, platform and payment method to localise the break.
Q4 — Your cohort's Day-1 retention is 40% but Day-30 is 4%. What story does that tell?
N-day retention
Read the curve: steep early drop then flat = users try it once and don't form a habit — an engagement/match problem, not acquisition.
Dig: where in those 30 days does the curve fall off a cliff? That's the drop-off point to fix.
Fix upstream: better onboarding, content match, and re-engagement nudges — the leaky-bucket holes.

Key Takeaways

1
Let the funnel do the thinking

Describe → Goal → walk the journey → define a metric at each stage. You never memorise a metric; you read the right one off the funnel.

2
Retention is lagging — measure upstream

It appears late in the funnel, so acquisition, onboarding and engagement metrics expose the leaks you must patch to protect it.

3
N-day retention = active on day N ÷ Day-0 cohort

Day 0 is each user's own start date. It's a direct read on product quality and stickiness.

4
Split a bank app into revenue + adoption

Transactions/day, per customer, total value for money; ported-vs-new users and accounts-via-app for adoption.

5
Separate new revenue from shifted revenue

Channel shift isn't growth. Use NPS, sentiment, Abandon Rate and Task Completion Rate to judge the experience itself.
